Most series-planning advice in indie publishing was written for genres where readers attach to the author. Power fantasy readers attach to the *world*, and a lot of inherited wisdom quietly breaks on that difference.

A new Series Launch Strategy report in the Market Analysis library on [LitRPGTools.com](https://litrpgtools.com) measured what actually happens across **5,013 qualified books in 2,258 series** on Amazon US Kindle, position by position. Rather than restate the whole thing, here are the three planning habits its evidence argues against, ranked by how much they cost an author who keeps them.

A note on the data first, in the report's own terms: demand is a directional Vault estimate, not KDP sales; continuation is a survivor measure across mature series, not individual read-through; editorial confidence is rated moderate. Good enough to correct a habit. Not a prophecy.

## 1. Planning the series as a trilogy

The most expensive mistake, because it's made before the first word.

The trilogy instinct assumes demand collapses after Book 3, so you plan a tidy three-book run and a fresh start. The report's headline says otherwise: by Book 6, a surviving series in this genre still carries **roughly two-thirds** of its own Book 1's estimated launch demand. That's the shape of the market, not an outlier; the typical surviving series still has meaningful launch demand at Books 4 through 6, and deeper entries can hold excellent rank.

The report's phrase for the risk is exact: the commercial danger is *stopping a healthy series too early*, not merely failing to start something new. Plan enough runway to reach at least Books 4–6. Plan a trilogy and you've capped a series the market would have carried further.

## 2. Treating Book 3 as the finish line

Second, and it compounds the first.

Even authors who don't hard-cap at three tend to use Book 3 as a decision point: keep going or stop. The report reframes it. Book 3 is where you **seed the next series**: announce it, prepare it, plant the crossover, while the core series keeps riding. The strongest authors in the data keep the brand warm by starting the next runway before the current one goes cold.

That's a different mental model. Book 3 isn't a fork between *continue* and *quit*; it's the point where you're running two tracks: the deep core series, and the early runway for what comes after it.

## 3. Deciding by gut instead of against a benchmark

Third, cheapest to fix, and the one the report is really built for.

Until now an author judging a Book 2 launch had a rank, a feeling, and a Facebook group. The report adds what it calls minimum rank objectives: for each book depth, a genre-typical first-month launch-rank target, a review band, and a red line below which it says to stop and reassess before writing the next one. Between target and line, the advice is to look at packaging, cadence, promotion, and your private read-through before deciding.

Those thresholds are specific numbers. They stay in the report; it's free to read on the platform, and the decision lines deserve their caveats attached. The point is that the benchmark exists. Nobody in this genre has to guess whether a launch was normal anymore.

## What the report holds back on purpose

Two things, and both are right. The book-by-book evidence table (typical launch rank, demand ratio, and continuation for each of the first six positions) is the report's core work, so read it there rather than as a screenshot here. And the per-series scoring, where an author or publisher sees their *own* series plotted against the decision lines, is a private member view. The market pattern is public; your numbers are yours.

## The bottom line

Ranked by cost: don't plan a trilogy, don't treat Book 3 as the exit, and don't judge a launch without a benchmark. The [long-running series readers rate highest](/rankings/top-power-fantasy-books/) went deep because the market rewarded depth, and the data now says so plainly.
