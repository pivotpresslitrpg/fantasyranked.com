Healing someone you love is a more intimate demonstration of magic than destroying a distant enemy. The Earth-side material in *Theater of War* gives Ty's abilities that kind of meaning. His grandmother's hearing is not an abstract attribute; it is part of an existing life.

Aaron Renfroe's second *Resonance Cycle* book also makes protection finite. Ty cannot simply announce that everyone important will be safe. He has to decide how promises become arrangements that might actually hold.

## The uncomfortable side of being able to help

Limited protection creates an ethical problem that growing power does not immediately remove. Whom do you include? What do you tell people? How do you prepare them for a threat they have not experienced?

Those questions make Ty's planning feel less like private optimisation and more like an obligation to others. They also expose the limits of treating relationships as tasks that can be completed and checked off.

## When the world gets uglier

The material involving divine authority and Sanctuary pushes against any comfortable assumption that magic arrives inside a trustworthy moral order. Ty's patron can impose conditions; powerful beings can make arrangements that look different from the perspective of those caught in them.

The possible weakness is emotional distance. Administrative problems and explanations can crowd out the interactions that make a rescue matter. Readers who want every gain to translate immediately into action may also find the deliberation slow.

For those interested in responsibility, though, this sequel has a clear attraction. It asks what changes when a careful young man can genuinely help people, yet still cannot help everyone. That is a stronger source of tension than simply giving him a bigger opponent.
