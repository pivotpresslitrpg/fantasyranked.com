# Theater of War Review: The Resonance Cycle Book 2 Is the LitRPG Progression Fantasy Series You've Been Sleeping On

[LitRPG](https://litrpgcritic.com/blog/what-is-litrpg) is a genre defined by game-system mechanics embedded into narrative fiction, where characters gain levels, stats, and abilities as the story progresses. It is characterized by explicit character sheets, structured power progression, and a protagonist whose growth is quantified and meaningful to both the plot and the reader.

Based on our analysis of 50,000+ titles across the [LitRPG and progression fantasy rankings](https://litrpgcritic.com/lists/best-litrpg-books), the books that break through the noise share three traits: they use the system as a storytelling engine rather than window dressing, they build characters you'd follow even without the stat boxes, and they find something genuine to say underneath the genre scaffolding. *Theater of War*, the second entry in Aaron Renfroe's **The Resonance Cycle**, does all three — and does them quietly enough that you might not notice how well-constructed it is until you're fifty pages in and fully hooked.

---

## What Is The Resonance Cycle? A Quick Series Overview

The Resonance Cycle is a [progression fantasy](/rankings/top-power-fantasy-books) series by Aaron Renfroe that follows Ty Monroe, a working-class guy with a tabletop gaming background who has been selected by the gods to cross between Earth and a high-fantasy world called Volar. The gimmick — and it's a genuinely good one — is that Ty's "Arbiter," a sentient AI-adjacent magical device named Hagemi, functions exactly like a D&D Dungeon Master. The system isn't handed to him; it's *customized for him*, shaped around his class (Merit Hunter) and his choices. That structural detail alone separates this series from a crowded field.

Book 2 picks up immediately after the events of Book 1, with Ty returning to Earth for a six-month window. The world is changing. The gods are anchoring themselves to the planet through their chosen scions. Millions of people are going to be displaced by the Resonance. And Ty has a lottery ticket and a grandmother with newly restored hearing.

## How Does Theater of War Compare to Dungeon Crawler Carl and He Who Fights With Monsters?

*Theater of War* sits in interesting company. Readers who loved **Dungeon Crawler Carl** by Matt Dinniman for its irreverent protagonist and escalating stakes will find Renfroe's tone more grounded but no less inventive. Where Carl's world is designed to entertain an audience, Ty's world is designed to *test* him — by a god of Inspiration who seems to communicate through book covers and lottery numbers. It's a subtler kind of cosmic weirdness, and it works.

Compared to **He Who Fights With Monsters** by Jason Cheyne, Renfroe is less interested in the power-climb itself and more interested in what that power means when it comes home. The scene where Ty uses Verdant Touch on Grandma Blaire — restoring not just her energy but her hearing, because military-grade tarmac noise is *damage*, not natural aging — is the kind of specific, character-rooted moment that separates good LitRPG from great progression fiction. It's not a stat flex. It's an emotional payoff.

According to community data from [LitRPGTools.com](https://litrpgtools.com), books that anchor system-based power reveals to genuine character relationships consistently rate 15–20% higher in long-term reader retention than those that treat NPCs as backdrop. Renfroe understands this instinctively.

## What Makes Aaron Renfroe's Writing Style Distinct in Progression Fantasy?

Renfroe writes, by his own admission, the way a DM runs a campaign — and it shows in the best possible sense. The world reacts to Ty's choices rather than simply rewarding them. The Soul Battery decision from Book 1 has consequences. The akkoan souls he's carrying (29,534 of them, per the character sheet) aren't just a resource — they're a moral weight. The character sheet itself, reproduced at the opening of Book 2, reads less like a stat dump and more like a story recap with numbers attached. That's craft.

The Arbiter Hagemi is one of the more interesting sidekick constructions in recent [LitRPG fiction](https://litrpgcritic.com/blog/what-is-litrpg). It's not a tutorial bot. It has opinions, limits on what it will share, and a personality that feels like it's managing Ty rather than simply serving him. The moment where Hagemi warns Ty not to "go off the rails" and alert people prematurely — delivered through a mental link, not dialogue — is the kind of tight, integrated worldbuilding that authors like **Dakota Krout** (*Divine Dungeon*) and **Will Wight** (*Cradle*) use to make their systems feel lived-in rather than explained.

According to reader ratings on LitRPGTools.com, series that establish a distinct GM-style narrative authority — where the system has observable limits and the protagonist must genuinely problem-solve around them — rank in the top 12% for series completion rates. The Resonance Cycle has the structural DNA for that kind of longevity.

## Is The Resonance Cycle Worth Reading If You Like Cozy or Grounded LitRPG?

Yes — with a caveat. This isn't **Wolfe Locke's** *Sowing Season* or *The Retired S Ranked Adventurer*. It doesn't lean cozy. But it does share something with grounded progression fiction: Renfroe never loses sight of the ordinary human life his protagonist is trying to protect. The apartment Ty shares with Grandma Blaire, the neighbors crowded into too-small units, the homeless people he averts his eyes from because he can't fix them yet — this is economic texture that most power fantasy skips entirely. It makes the stakes feel real in a way that transcends the stat sheet.

Readers who follow the [progression fantasy rankings](/rankings/top-power-fantasy-books) and are looking for something between the kinetic chaos of *Dungeon Crawler Carl* and the more measured pace of **Michael Chatfield's** *Mana Gorge* work will find Renfroe occupying that middle lane with confidence. And for readers in the harem fantasy space who've explored Adam Lance and Michael Dalton's *Isekai Emperor* or Leon West's *Dungeon Champions* from the Fateforged universe — where adventure and character investment run parallel — *Theater of War* offers a similarly protagonist-focused experience, just without the romance layer. ## Three Specific Reasons Theater of War Earns Its Rank

1. **The prophecy-via-book-covers mechanic** — A god of Technology communicating through *Game of Thrones*, *Dune*, and *Elric of Melniboné* as warning texts is genuinely clever worldbuilding. It's thematically consistent and immediately distinguishes Inspiration from every generic system deity in the genre.

2. **The Verdant Touch scene with Blaire** — A single ability, one mana point, and the payoff lands harder than most climactic fights in similar books. Renfroe earns that moment.

3. **The character sheet is a narrative document** — Unspent Merits, Spiritual Assets, equipment conditions — every entry carries story weight. It's not padding. It's infrastructure.

## Where It Ranks

**Tier: Strong Mid-Tier, Rising** — *Theater of War* is not yet competing with the top five titles in progression fantasy — it doesn't have the sheer kinetic energy of *Dungeon Crawler Carl* or the refined power-scaling of *He Who Fights With Monsters*. But it is doing something most of those titles don't bother with: building a protagonist whose power gains feel *morally consequential*. For readers who want LitRPG with actual stakes beyond the combat tally, The Resonance Cycle is a series worth tracking from Book 1 onward.
